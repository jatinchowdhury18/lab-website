Over the past 8 years in academia and industry, I've spent the vast
majority of my working time writing code. Along the way, I've developed
some fairly strong opinions about good and bad ways to write code,
and I've collected a reasonably large supply of educational resources.
The purpose of this post is to collect those resources in one organized
place.

## System Architecture

Casey Muratori has been massively influential on my understanding of
system architecture, and many of my formative opinions on the subject
have come from his "Handmade Hero" and "Performance-Aware
Programming" series.

![The Big OOPs](https://youtu.be/wo84LFzx5nI)

"The Big OOPs" describes the history of object-oriented architecture,
and along the way uncovers a 35-year gap between the first usage of
an ["entity-component system"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entity_component_system)
in 1963(!) and it's re-discovery in 1998.

![Becoming an N+2 Programmer](https://youtu.be/xt1KNDmOYqA)

I could have easily picked 7 or 8 clips from "Handmade Hero", but this
clip is arguably my favorite. Casey points out the complexity that
comes with common system architectures for things like memory
allocation and error handling, and encourages the programmer to
think more in terms of "groups" of things rather than "individual" things.

## Data-Oriented Design

![Data-Oriented Design and C++](https://youtu.be/rX0ItVEVjHc)

Mike Acton's 2014 CppCon keynote has achieved legendary status amongst
some classes of programmers, and for good reason. Along with laying
out the groundwork and implications of successful data-oriented
programming, Mike has a great philosophy of treating computer
science as a proper "science" where claims can be tested and
verified on the target hardware.

![Intro to Data Oriented Design for Games](https://youtu.be/WwkuAqObplU)

Nic Barker gives a somewhat friendlier introduction to data-oriented
programming. In particular, I love his analogy for the various layers
of data caches and how he builds an intuition for how long memory access
takes across the different layers.

## Multi-Threaded Programming

Multi-threaded programming is a massive topic in its own right.
This list is a bit heavier on things that matter a lot for
the types of software that I write, notably lock-free and
wait-free programming.

![The Cost of Concurrency Coordination](https://youtu.be/tND-wBBZ8RY)

Jon's talk for Jane Street breaks down the relationship between
CPU cache lines and cross-core communication that even "atomic"
operations typically require. He also introduces a clever
"left-right" algorithm that allows wait-free "read" operations
to parallelize at scale.

![Parallelizing the Physics Solver](https://youtu.be/Kvsvd67XUKw)

Dennis Gustafsson presents a specific parallel programming
problem, and gives great insight into profiling the
bottlenecks and arriving at an efficient solution.

<div class="substack-post-embed"><p lang="en">Multi-Core By Default by Ryan Fleury</p><p>On multi-core programming, not as a special-case technique, but as a new dimension in all code.</p><a data-post-link href="https://www.dgtlgrove.com/p/multi-core-by-default">Read on Substack</a></div><script async src="https://substack.com/embedjs/embed.js" charset="utf-8"></script>

Ryan Fleury talks through parallelism more at the architecture
level. Incidentally, the design that he arrives at is quite
similar to the ["thread pool"](https://github.com/free-audio/clap/blob/main/include/clap/ext/thread-pool.h)
mechanism present in the CLAP plugin standard.

![Thread Synchronization in Real-Time Audio Processing with RCU](https://youtu.be/7fKxIZOyBCE)

Timur Doumler is really the person to listen to when it comes
to real-time programming in C++ for audio applications. I could
have picked a number of his blog posts or conference talks, but
I really like the RCU algorithm thta he presents here, and it's
been quite influential on my own work.

![Wait-Free Programming from Scratch](https://youtu.be/A9Gf0wFz3kQ)

Speaking of... This is a talk that I gave for the Northwest C++
Users Group discussing the development of a wait-free algorithm
from first principles. This talk was _heavily_ influenced by Timur
and Jon's talks linked above.

## Coding Style

When I was learning to program ["Clean Code"](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FX3CJGFX)
was all the rage. It took a while to de-program myself
from that mindset.

![Clean Code, Horrible Performance](https://youtu.be/tD5NrevFtbU)

Casey Muratori did a lot of that work in just 20 minutes,
demonstrating how commonly cited "clean coding" practices
could easily wipe out a decade or more of hardware performance
improvements.

![Admiral Grace Hopper Explains the Nanosecond](https://youtu.be/9eyFDBPk4Yw)

I spend a lot of time thinking about software performance, and perhaps
even more time trying to get other people to care. Grace Hopper presents
an obvious physical reminder of "what programmers are throwing away,
when they throw away microseconds".

![Preventing the Collapse of Civilization](https://youtu.be/ZSRHeXYDLko)

Technology occupies a precarious position in our current
civilization. While our society depends ever more on
technology, there are massive problems with supply chains,
overly centralized knowledge, and _especially_ complexity
that could lead to a civilization-level collapse, as seen
during the Bronze Age and other times throughout history.

Building up fundamental understanding and cultures
of engineering excellence can go a long way towards
stabilizing our relationship with technology... Even
in seemingly non-serious areas like audio and games
programming.
